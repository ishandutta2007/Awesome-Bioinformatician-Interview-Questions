# 1. Molecular Biology & Genetics Fundamentals

The biological grounding that any bioinformatics work actually needs to be built on — a bioinformatician who only understands the algorithms without the underlying biology will eventually make consequential mistakes.

---

### 1.1 🟢 What is the central dogma of molecular biology, and why does a bioinformatician need to understand it beyond a purely conceptual level?

The central dogma describes the general flow of genetic information: DNA is transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into protein. A bioinformatician needs to understand this beyond a purely conceptual level because it directly determines what kind of data and analysis is appropriate for a given biological question — whether you're working with genomic DNA sequence, transcript-level RNA data, or protein sequence data changes which reference databases, coordinate systems, and analytical tools are actually applicable, and misunderstanding where in this flow a specific dataset or question sits (for instance, conflating a genomic coordinate with a transcript coordinate, or not accounting for the fact that a single gene can produce multiple different transcript isoforms) is a common and consequential source of analysis errors for people without solid grounding in this fundamental framework.

### 1.2 🟢 What is the difference between a gene, a transcript, and an exon, and why does this distinction matter for something as basic as counting how many "genes" are expressed in an RNA-seq experiment?

A gene is a genomic locus (a defined region of DNA) that can give rise to one or more functional products; a transcript is a specific RNA molecule produced from a gene through transcription, and a single gene can produce multiple different transcript isoforms through alternative splicing or alternative transcription start/end sites; an exon is a specific segment of a gene's sequence that's retained in the mature, spliced transcript (as opposed to introns, which are removed during splicing). This distinction matters directly for something as seemingly basic as counting expressed genes in an RNA-seq experiment because sequencing reads are generated from transcripts, not directly from genes, meaning a gene-level expression estimate typically requires aggregating read counts across all of that gene's possible transcript isoforms, and different analytical choices about how to handle this aggregation (and how to handle reads that map ambiguously across multiple overlapping genes or isoforms) can produce meaningfully different results — a bioinformatician needs to understand exactly what level (gene, transcript, or exon) a given analysis or tool is actually operating at, rather than treating "expression" as a single, unambiguous concept.

### 1.3 🟡 What is the difference between a SNP, an indel, and a structural variant, and why do these different variant types typically require genuinely different detection algorithms rather than a single, universal variant-calling approach?

A SNP (single nucleotide polymorphism/variant) is a single-base difference from a reference sequence; an indel is a small insertion or deletion of one or more bases; a structural variant is a larger-scale genomic alteration (typically defined as affecting 50 base pairs or more), including large deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations. These different variant types typically require genuinely different detection algorithms because they leave fundamentally different signatures in sequencing data — a SNP is detectable through straightforward comparison of aligned read bases against the reference at a single position, a small indel requires the alignment algorithm to correctly handle a local insertion or deletion without misaligning the surrounding sequence, and a large structural variant is often too big to be captured within the span of a single sequencing read at all, requiring detection through indirect signals instead — read-pair distance and orientation anomalies, split-read alignments, or read-depth changes across a region — meaning a bioinformatician needs to select or combine specifically appropriate tools for the variant size and type actually relevant to a given analysis, rather than assuming a single variant caller optimized for one variant class will reliably detect all of them.

### 1.4 🟢 What is the difference between the coding and non-coding regions of the genome, and why has the field's understanding of non-coding DNA's functional importance changed substantially over time?

Coding regions are the portions of the genome that are translated into protein sequence, comprising a relatively small fraction of the total human genome (roughly 1-2%). Non-coding regions include everything else — introns, regulatory sequences (promoters, enhancers), non-coding RNA genes, and large stretches of sequence with no currently well-characterized function. The field's understanding of non-coding DNA's functional importance has changed substantially over time — earlier characterizations sometimes referred to non-coding DNA dismissively (a framing now considered outdated and overly simplistic), while subsequent research, including large-scale functional annotation efforts, has established that a considerable portion of non-coding sequence plays genuine, important regulatory roles (controlling when, where, and how much a gene is expressed) or produces functional non-coding RNA molecules — a bioinformatician should understand this history partly because it's directly relevant to interpreting variant significance (a variant in a non-coding regulatory region can be just as functionally consequential as a coding variant, even though non-coding variant interpretation remains generally less mature and more difficult than coding variant interpretation) and partly as a useful, concrete example of how quickly foundational understanding in this field can evolve.

### 1.5 🟡 What is alternative splicing, and why does it create a genuine analytical challenge for accurately quantifying gene and transcript expression from short-read RNA-seq data?

Alternative splicing is the process by which a single gene's pre-mRNA transcript can be spliced in different ways to produce multiple distinct mature mRNA transcripts (differing in which exons are included, or in alternative splice site usage), allowing a single gene to encode multiple different protein products or transcript variants. This creates a genuine analytical challenge for short-read RNA-seq quantification specifically because a typical short sequencing read (commonly 100-150 base pairs) is often too short to span enough of a transcript's sequence to unambiguously determine which specific isoform it originated from, particularly for reads falling within exons shared across multiple isoforms of the same gene — meaning accurately quantifying isoform-level (as opposed to gene-level) expression from short-read data generally requires probabilistic inference methods that model the likelihood of a given read's isoform of origin based on the pattern of reads across the whole transcript, rather than being a matter of simply and directly counting reads unambiguously assignable to a single isoform, and this is a meaningfully harder and less certain analytical problem than gene-level quantification, which can more often tolerate this isoform ambiguity by aggregating across all of a gene's isoforms.

### 1.6 🟢 What is the difference between homozygous and heterozygous, and why does correctly determining zygosity matter for both variant calling accuracy and downstream biological interpretation?

An individual is homozygous at a given genomic position if both of their inherited chromosome copies carry the same allele at that position, and heterozygous if the two copies carry different alleles. Correctly determining zygosity matters for variant calling accuracy because a genuine heterozygous variant should be supported by roughly (though not exactly) half of the sequencing reads covering that position, while a homozygous variant should be supported by nearly all covering reads, and a variant caller uses this expected proportion as part of its statistical model for distinguishing genuine variants from sequencing errors or artifacts — a systematic bias or error in this determination can affect both the confidence of a variant call and its correct classification. It matters for downstream biological interpretation because zygosity is directly relevant to inheritance pattern analysis (per the recessive versus dominant inheritance concepts discussed in adjacent precision medicine literature) and to correctly identifying compound heterozygous findings, where two different variants on opposite chromosome copies of the same gene combine to produce a phenotype that neither variant alone would cause.

### 1.7 🟡 What is linkage disequilibrium, and why is this population genetics concept directly relevant to a bioinformatician's practical work in genome-wide association analysis and variant imputation?

Linkage disequilibrium refers to the non-random association between alleles at different genomic positions, arising because variants located physically close together on a chromosome tend to be co-inherited across generations rather than assorting independently, as discussed in more depth in adjacent precision medicine genomics literature. This is directly relevant to practical bioinformatics work in several ways — in genome-wide association analysis, a statistically significant association at a specific genetic marker doesn't necessarily identify the actual causal variant, since the marker may simply be in linkage disequilibrium with (and therefore co-inherited alongside) the true causal variant elsewhere in the same region, meaning fine-mapping and follow-up analysis is generally needed to move from a statistical association to a specific causal hypothesis; and in genotype imputation (a common practical technique for inferring genotypes at positions not directly measured by a given genotyping array, based on their linkage disequilibrium relationship with directly-measured nearby positions and a reference haplotype panel), the accuracy of imputed genotypes depends directly on how well the specific reference panel used captures the linkage disequilibrium patterns relevant to the population being studied.

### 1.8 🟢 What is a reference genome, and what practical limitations does relying on a single reference genome assembly introduce into genomic analysis?

A reference genome is a standardized, representative genomic sequence assembly (such as the widely-used GRCh38 human assembly) used as the common coordinate system and baseline for aligning sequencing reads and reporting variants, rather than every analysis needing to work from a completely de novo assembly each time. Practical limitations include that any single reference assembly, however carefully constructed, doesn't represent the true genomic diversity across a species' population — it's effectively a composite or mosaic sequence, and regions of high population-level structural variability, or regions poorly represented in the specific individuals whose DNA contributed to the reference assembly, can be systematically harder to align reads to accurately, which has motivated more recent pangenome reference efforts incorporating sequence from a more diverse set of individuals specifically to reduce this kind of reference bias, echoing similar reference representativeness concerns discussed in adjacent precision medicine genomics literature.

### 1.9 🟡 What is the difference between a genome assembly's "reference" and "alternate" (or "alt") contigs, and why does correctly handling alt contigs matter for avoiding a specific, well-documented category of read-mapping artifact?

The primary or reference assembly represents a single, consensus representation of each chromosomal region, while alt contigs represent additional, alternative sequence representations for specific genomic regions known to have significant structural variability or complexity across the population (such as certain immune-related gene clusters), included in modern reference assemblies specifically to better capture this variability rather than forcing every individual's sequence into a single, potentially poorly-fitting representation. Correctly handling alt contigs matters for avoiding a specific, well-documented mapping artifact because if an alignment pipeline isn't specifically configured to properly recognize and appropriately handle reads that could plausibly originate from either the primary assembly region or its corresponding alt contig, reads can be incorrectly mapped with inflated confidence to one representation when they're actually ambiguous between the two, or coverage and variant calling in these regions can become systematically distorted — a bioinformatician working with a modern reference assembly including alt contigs needs to specifically confirm their alignment and downstream tools are alt-aware and correctly configured, rather than assuming a pipeline originally validated against an older, alt-contig-free reference will behave correctly without modification.

### 1.10 🟢 What is a codon, and why does understanding the genetic code's redundancy (multiple codons encoding the same amino acid) matter for correctly interpreting the functional consequence of a coding variant?

A codon is a sequence of three nucleotides that specifies a particular amino acid (or a stop signal) during translation, and the genetic code is redundant (sometimes called "degenerate") in the sense that most amino acids are specified by more than one possible codon. Understanding this redundancy matters for correctly interpreting a coding variant's functional consequence because a single-nucleotide change within a codon doesn't automatically change the encoded amino acid — a synonymous (or silent) variant changes the DNA sequence without changing the resulting amino acid, due to this codon redundancy, while a missense variant does change the encoded amino acid, and a nonsense variant changes a codon into a premature stop signal — a bioinformatician annotating variant consequences needs to correctly apply this genetic code logic (accounting for the correct reading frame and strand) to accurately classify a given coding variant into the correct functional category, since this classification directly affects downstream prioritization and interpretation.

### 1.11 🟡 What is epigenetics, and what specific molecular measurements does a bioinformatician typically need to work with when analyzing epigenetic data, distinct from the DNA sequence variant data discussed elsewhere in this category?

Epigenetics refers to heritable or stable changes in gene expression that don't involve changes to the underlying DNA sequence itself, commonly mediated through mechanisms including DNA methylation (chemical modification of DNA, typically at CpG sites, that can affect nearby gene expression) and histone modification (chemical changes to the proteins DNA is packaged around, affecting chromatin accessibility and gene expression). A bioinformatician analyzing epigenetic data typically works with data types genuinely distinct from DNA sequence variant data — bisulfite sequencing data (a specific sequencing chemistry that converts unmethylated cytosines to a different base while leaving methylated cytosines unchanged, allowing methylation status to be inferred from the resulting sequence differences) for methylation analysis, and ChIP-seq or ATAC-seq data (sequencing reads specifically enriched for regions bound by a particular protein, or for regions of accessible, open chromatin, respectively) for chromatin state analysis — these data types require genuinely distinct alignment and analysis approaches (bisulfite-converted reads, for instance, require alignment algorithms specifically designed to handle the resulting sequence conversion, rather than standard DNA alignment tools) that a bioinformatician needs specific familiarity with beyond standard DNA sequence variant analysis.

### 1.12 🟢 What is the difference between a prokaryotic and eukaryotic genome from a bioinformatics analysis perspective, and what specific practical differences does this create for gene annotation and genome assembly work?

Prokaryotic genomes (bacteria and archaea) are typically smaller, generally lack introns (meaning genes are typically continuous, uninterrupted coding sequences), often have genes organized into operons (clusters of functionally related genes transcribed together as a single unit), and typically have a much higher proportion of their genome directly coding for protein compared to eukaryotic genomes. Eukaryotic genomes are typically larger, contain introns requiring the splicing process discussed in category 1.2 and 1.5, have a much lower proportion of coding sequence relative to total genome size, and often contain substantial repetitive sequence. These differences create genuinely distinct practical challenges for gene annotation and assembly — prokaryotic genome annotation is generally more computationally tractable given the simpler, more continuous gene structure and higher gene density, while eukaryotic genome annotation needs to correctly identify intron-exon boundaries and handle the much lower overall coding density and higher repeat content, and eukaryotic genome assembly (particularly for larger, more repeat-rich genomes) is generally a substantially harder computational problem than prokaryotic genome assembly, meaning a bioinformatician needs to select tools and approaches appropriate to which specific type of organism they're actually working with rather than assuming a single, universal approach applies equally well to both.

### 1.13 🟡 What is horizontal gene transfer, and why is this phenomenon a particularly important consideration for bioinformatics analysis of microbial genomes and metagenomic data specifically, distinct from typical eukaryotic genomic analysis?

Horizontal gene transfer refers to the movement of genetic material between organisms through mechanisms other than traditional vertical inheritance from parent to offspring, a phenomenon that occurs at meaningfully higher rates in prokaryotes than in most eukaryotes and has significant implications for how genetic relationships and evolutionary history need to be interpreted in microbial genomics. This is a particularly important consideration for microbial and metagenomic bioinformatics analysis specifically because it means a gene's presence in a given bacterial genome doesn't necessarily reflect that organism's overall evolutionary lineage the way it more reliably would in eukaryotic genomics — a specific gene (such as an antibiotic resistance gene) can be acquired through horizontal transfer from a genuinely distantly related organism, meaning phylogenetic and comparative genomic analysis methods that assume a straightforward, tree-like pattern of vertical inheritance (appropriate for much of eukaryotic comparative genomics, discussed further in category 9) can produce misleading results if applied naively to microbial data without accounting for this phenomenon, and a bioinformatician working with microbial genomic or metagenomic data needs specific awareness of and appropriate methodological adjustment for this distinctly higher rate of horizontal transfer.

### 1.14 🟢 What is a pseudogene, and why does correctly distinguishing a pseudogene from its functional parent gene present a specific, recurring bioinformatics challenge in sequence alignment and variant calling?

A pseudogene is a genomic sequence that closely resembles a functional gene but has typically lost its ability to produce a functional protein product, often arising through gene duplication followed by the accumulation of disabling mutations, or through retrotransposition of a processed mRNA back into the genome. Correctly distinguishing a pseudogene from its functional parent gene presents a specific, recurring bioinformatics challenge because their high sequence similarity means sequencing reads originating from either the functional gene or its pseudogene copy can be difficult to confidently and uniquely map to the correct one, creating exactly the kind of multi-mapping ambiguity discussed in adjacent precision medicine genomics literature regarding genes with closely related paralogs — a bioinformatician analyzing a gene known to have one or more closely related pseudogenes needs specific awareness of this risk, since naive variant calling in this kind of region can produce spurious or unreliable results if reads from the pseudogene are incorrectly attributed to the functional gene (or vice versa), sometimes requiring specialized alignment or analysis approaches specifically designed to handle these regions more carefully than standard analysis would.

### 1.15 🟡 What is the difference between orthologous and paralogous genes, and why is this distinction essential for correctly interpreting cross-species comparative genomic analysis?

Orthologous genes are genes in different species that descended from a single ancestral gene present in their most recent common ancestor, typically (though not always) retaining a similar function across the species being compared, having diverged through speciation events. Paralogous genes are genes that arose through a gene duplication event, which may have occurred within a single species' lineage (and can therefore exist as multiple paralogous copies within the same genome, as with the pseudogene-parent gene relationship discussed in category 1.14) or before a speciation event (meaning the resulting paralogs are then also found, each independently, across the descendant species). This distinction is essential for correctly interpreting cross-species comparative genomic analysis because comparing paralogous rather than truly orthologous genes across species when attempting to establish evolutionary relationships or infer conserved function can lead to seriously misleading conclusions — a bioinformatician performing comparative genomic or phylogenetic analysis (discussed further in category 9) needs to use appropriate orthology inference methods to correctly identify true orthologous relationships before drawing conclusions about conserved function or evolutionary history, rather than assuming that the most sequence-similar gene found in a different species is automatically the correct orthologous counterpart, since a closely related paralog can sometimes be more sequence-similar than the true ortholog.

### 1.16 🟢 What is the difference between genotype and phenotype, and why does a bioinformatician need to maintain a clear, disciplined distinction between "what a variant is predicted to do" and "what has actually been observed to happen" when working with genomic data?

Genotype refers to an organism's specific genetic makeup at a given locus or across its genome; phenotype refers to the organism's actual observable traits or characteristics, which arise from the complex interaction between genotype and environmental and other non-genetic factors. A bioinformatician needs to maintain a clear, disciplined distinction between genotype-based prediction and observed phenotype because a genomic analysis pipeline typically works directly with genotype data and computational predictions about a variant's likely functional or phenotypic consequence, but these predictions — however well-grounded in current understanding — remain distinct from and shouldn't be conflated with actual observed phenotypic outcomes, echoing the broader evidentiary discipline discussed in adjacent precision medicine variant interpretation literature regarding not over-interpreting computational predictions as equivalent to direct clinical or functional evidence — this distinction matters practically for how confidently a bioinformatician should communicate findings, particularly in any context where genomic analysis results might inform a real clinical or research decision.

### 1.17 🟡 What is genomic imprinting, and why does this phenomenon create a specific analytical complication for variant interpretation that standard, allele-agnostic genotype calling doesn't automatically account for?

Genomic imprinting is an epigenetic phenomenon in which a gene's expression depends specifically on which parent it was inherited from, with one parental copy being epigenetically silenced while the other remains active, in contrast to the more typical pattern where both inherited copies of a gene are expressed (though not necessarily at exactly equal levels). This creates a specific analytical complication because standard genotype calling from sequencing data typically reports a variant's presence without distinguishing which specific parental chromosome copy it's located on (unless specific phasing information, discussed in adjacent precision medicine literature regarding compound heterozygous variant interpretation, is available) — for a gene subject to imprinting, a variant's actual functional and phenotypic consequence can depend critically on whether it's located on the expressed or silenced parental copy, meaning standard, parent-of-origin-agnostic variant interpretation can be genuinely misleading for genes known to be subject to imprinting, and a bioinformatician working on a phenotype or gene region with known imprinting needs specific additional analysis (often requiring parental sample data, or specialized methylation-based approaches to infer parent-of-origin) beyond standard genotype calling to correctly interpret a variant's likely functional relevance.

### 1.18 🟢 What is a multiple sequence alignment, and why is aligning three or more sequences simultaneously a computationally and conceptually distinct problem from the pairwise sequence alignment discussed in category 2, rather than simply an extension of the same approach?

A multiple sequence alignment arranges three or more biological sequences (DNA, RNA, or protein) so that regions of similarity across all the sequences are aligned together in shared columns, typically used to identify conserved regions, infer evolutionary relationships, or build a model of a sequence family's characteristic pattern. This is a computationally and conceptually distinct problem from pairwise alignment (discussed in category 2) rather than simply an extension of the same approach because the optimal alignment algorithms used for pairwise alignment (which can guarantee finding the mathematically optimal alignment for two sequences in a computationally tractable way) don't scale efficiently to three or more sequences — finding the truly optimal multiple sequence alignment is computationally intractable for anything beyond a small number of sequences, meaning essentially all practical multiple sequence alignment tools rely on heuristic approaches (commonly progressive alignment strategies, which build up a multiple alignment by iteratively combining pairwise or smaller-group alignments guided by an estimated evolutionary relationship tree) that produce a good, but not guaranteed-optimal, alignment — a bioinformatician needs to understand that a multiple sequence alignment tool's output is a heuristic approximation, not a mathematically guaranteed best answer, which has real implications for how much confidence should be placed in specific, subtle alignment details, particularly in more divergent or difficult-to-align sequence regions.

### 1.19 🟡 What is the concept of sequence conservation, and how is conservation across species commonly used as a specific line of evidence for identifying functionally important genomic regions, including in non-coding sequence where direct functional annotation is often unavailable?

Sequence conservation refers to the degree to which a specific genomic region's sequence has remained similar across different species over evolutionary time, generally interpreted as reflecting purifying selection against mutations in that region — since a region under no functional constraint would be expected to accumulate sequence changes over evolutionary time roughly at the background mutation rate, while a functionally important region under selective constraint would be expected to show a slower rate of sequence change, since mutations disrupting its function would tend to be selected against. Conservation across species is commonly used as an important, complementary line of evidence for identifying functionally important genomic regions specifically because it can be computed directly from multiple sequence alignment data (discussed in category 1.18) across many species without requiring direct experimental functional characterization of the region in question, making it particularly valuable for the vast stretches of non-coding sequence discussed in category 1.4 where direct functional annotation remains limited — a bioinformatician should understand conservation scores (commonly derived from multi-species genome alignments) as a genuinely useful but indirect and imperfect line of evidence, since a region can be functionally important without necessarily showing strong cross-species conservation (if its function is genuinely lineage-specific), and conversely, high conservation doesn't itself specify exactly what function a given region actually serves.

### 1.20 🔴 A colleague describes a novel variant as "likely deleterious" based solely on the fact that it falls within a highly evolutionarily conserved genomic region. What's incomplete about relying on conservation alone for this kind of functional call, and how would you extend this reasoning to a more rigorous evaluation?

Conservation alone provides genuinely useful but importantly incomplete evidence for a functional consequence call, and I'd want to extend this reasoning along several specific lines before treating "likely deleterious" as an adequately supported conclusion. First, conservation indicates that a region is likely under some form of selective constraint and therefore likely functionally important in some capacity, but it doesn't by itself specify what a specific variant within that region actually does to that function — a variant could fall within a highly conserved region while itself being a relatively conservative, low-impact change (a synonymous coding change, per category 1.10, or a change to a less functionally critical specific position within a broader conserved element) that doesn't meaningfully disrupt the region's function despite the region's overall conservation. Second, per the genotype-versus-phenotype discipline discussed in category 1.16, "likely deleterious" implies an actual phenotypic consequence, which conservation-based computational prediction alone cannot establish — this kind of prediction should be explicitly framed as one piece of supporting, generally moderate-strength computational evidence (directly echoing the evidentiary weighting discussion in adjacent precision medicine variant interpretation literature, which specifically treats computational predictions as supporting rather than strong standalone evidence) rather than a sufficient, standalone basis for a confident deleteriousness claim. I'd want to specifically check whether additional, complementary evidence is available or could be sought — population frequency data (is this variant unexpectedly common in population databases, which would argue against a highly deleterious effect), any available functional or experimental data specific to this variant or gene, and, where the biological context allows it, family or cohort segregation data — before characterizing the variant with the same confidence the "likely deleterious" framing implies, treating the conservation evidence as a reasonable starting point for further investigation rather than an adequate stopping point on its own.
