# 5. Statistics & Experimental Design

The statistical foundation underlying rigorous bioinformatics analysis — much of this echoes concepts developed elsewhere in this series, applied specifically to the kinds of study design and analysis questions a working bioinformatician encounters directly.

---

### 5.1 🟢 What is the difference between biological replicates and technical replicates, and why does confusing these two replicate types in a statistical analysis lead to a specific, serious error called pseudoreplication?

Biological replicates are independent biological samples (different individual organisms, different independently-grown cell culture dishes, or similar genuinely independent biological units) intended to capture genuine biological variability between independent instances of the same experimental condition. Technical replicates are repeated measurements of the same underlying biological sample (such as sequencing the same RNA extraction multiple times, or running the same sample through a qPCR assay in multiple wells), intended to capture measurement or technical variability rather than genuine biological variability. Confusing these two replicate types leads to pseudoreplication — a serious statistical error where technical replicates are incorrectly treated as if they were independent biological replicates in a statistical analysis, artificially inflating the apparent sample size and correspondingly and misleadingly inflating the statistical confidence (reducing the standard error and producing artificially small p-values) beyond what the actual number of genuinely independent biological observations supports — a bioinformatician needs to correctly identify which specific replicate structure a given dataset actually has and ensure any statistical analysis appropriately reflects the true number of independent biological units, rather than treating technical replicate measurements as though they each contributed independent biological information.

### 5.2 🟡 What is statistical power, and what specific factors does a bioinformatician need to consider when advising a researcher on how many biological replicates a genomics experiment (such as an RNA-seq differential expression study) actually needs?

Statistical power is the probability that a study will correctly detect a genuine effect, if one truly exists, given the study's specific design and sample size, discussed in more general terms in adjacent precision medicine biostatistics literature. When advising on replicate numbers for a genomics experiment specifically, a bioinformatician needs to consider several concrete, specific factors — the expected magnitude of the biological effect being studied (a subtler expected expression change generally requires more replicates to reliably detect than a larger, more dramatic expected change), the expected biological variability within each experimental group (which, per the negative binomial modeling discussed in category 4.6, is a genuinely important and not always intuitive quantity for RNA-seq data specifically, since variability can differ substantially across different gene expression levels), the number of genes or features being simultaneously tested (since the multiple testing correction discussed in category 4.7 reduces effective statistical power compared to a single-hypothesis test), and the specific downstream statistical method planned for the analysis, since different methods can have somewhat different power characteristics for the same underlying data — a bioinformatician should be able to perform or reference an appropriate power calculation (increasingly supported by dedicated tools designed specifically for RNA-seq and other genomics experimental designs) rather than relying on a rule-of-thumb replicate number without any grounding in the actual expected effect size and variability relevant to the specific planned experiment.

### 5.3 🟢 What is confounding, and can you give a concrete example of how a poorly-designed genomics experiment could inadvertently confound a biological variable of interest with a technical or other uncontrolled variable?

Confounding occurs when a variable other than the one being studied is associated with both the exposure or condition of interest and the outcome being measured, creating a spurious or distorted apparent association that doesn't reflect a genuine, direct relationship between the variable of actual interest and the outcome — discussed in more detail in adjacent precision medicine biostatistics literature. A concrete genomics example, directly echoing the batch effect discussion in category 4.8: if a researcher processes all of their treated samples on one day using one batch of reagents, and all of their control samples on a different day using a different reagent batch, then "treatment" and "processing batch" become perfectly confounded — any observed expression difference between the groups could reflect the genuine treatment effect, a purely technical batch artifact, or some unknown combination of both, and no statistical analysis performed after the fact can actually disentangle these two possibilities from data collected this way, since the two variables vary in perfect lockstep with each other throughout the entire dataset — this is precisely why good experimental design (randomizing or balancing sample processing across batches with respect to the biological variable of interest) is fundamentally necessary and can't be adequately substituted for by any downstream statistical correction technique applied after an inadequately designed experiment has already been conducted.

### 5.4 🟡 What is the difference between a Type I and Type II error, and why might a bioinformatician need to think differently about the appropriate balance between these two error types depending on whether a given analysis is exploratory/hypothesis-generating versus confirmatory?

A Type I error (false positive) occurs when an analysis concludes a genuine effect or finding exists when none actually does; a Type II error (false negative) occurs when an analysis fails to detect a genuine effect that does actually exist, discussed in more general terms in adjacent precision medicine biostatistics literature. A bioinformatician needs to think differently about the appropriate balance between these error types depending on an analysis's purpose — for an exploratory or hypothesis-generating analysis (such as an initial differential expression screen intended to identify promising candidate genes for further, more targeted follow-up validation), a somewhat higher tolerance for Type I error (accepting more false positives among the flagged candidates) may be reasonable, since the flagged candidates will undergo further validation before any strong conclusion is drawn, and missing a genuinely important candidate (a Type II error) at this early, discovery stage could mean a promising finding is never pursued further at all; for a confirmatory analysis intended to provide definitive evidence supporting a specific, already-formulated hypothesis (particularly one that might inform a significant downstream decision or publication claim), a more conservative approach favoring lower Type I error is generally more appropriate, since the cost of a false-positive confirmatory claim is typically higher than the cost of requiring somewhat stronger evidence before declaring a confirmatory finding established — this distinction directly parallels similar reasoning about appropriate error-type balance discussed in adjacent precision medicine and longevity therapeutics biostatistics literature.

### 5.5 🟢 What is a p-value, and what is a common, consequential misinterpretation of what a p-value actually represents that a rigorous bioinformatician should be able to clearly correct?

A p-value is the probability of observing a result at least as extreme as the one actually obtained, assuming the null hypothesis (typically, that no genuine effect or difference exists) is true. A common, consequential misinterpretation is treating the p-value as though it directly represents the probability that the null hypothesis itself is true (or, conversely, the probability that the observed finding is genuinely real) — this is not what a p-value actually means, since it's calculated under the specific assumption that the null hypothesis is already true, and doesn't directly provide a probability statement about the null hypothesis's actual truth or falsity given the observed data at all — a rigorous bioinformatician should be able to clearly explain this distinction when it comes up (a p-value of 0.03 doesn't mean there's a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true, or a 97% chance the observed effect is genuinely real), and should be similarly clear that statistical significance (a small p-value) doesn't by itself establish that an effect is large, biologically meaningful, or practically important, echoing the statistical-versus-clinical-significance distinction discussed in more depth in adjacent precision medicine biostatistics literature.

### 5.6 🟡 What is the concept of a null distribution, and why do permutation-based statistical testing approaches (rather than relying solely on a standard, theoretical parametric distribution) become particularly valuable when a dataset's actual underlying statistical properties don't clearly match a standard theoretical distribution's assumptions?

A null distribution represents the range of results that would be expected to occur purely by chance under the null hypothesis, against which an actually observed result is compared to determine statistical significance — for many standard statistical tests, this null distribution is derived theoretically from a known, standard probability distribution (such as the normal or t-distribution) based on specific underlying assumptions about the data. Permutation-based testing instead empirically generates an approximate null distribution directly from the actual dataset itself, typically by repeatedly randomly shuffling or reassigning the group labels (or another relevant aspect of the data structure) many times and recomputing the test statistic for each random reshuffling, building up an empirical distribution of what the test statistic would look like under random chance given this specific dataset's actual characteristics, rather than assuming it matches a standard theoretical distribution's specific assumptions. This becomes particularly valuable when a dataset's actual statistical properties (its true underlying variance structure, or other characteristics) don't clearly or reliably match the assumptions a standard parametric theoretical test would require, which is a genuinely common situation in various bioinformatics contexts (complex genomic data structures, small sample sizes where the underlying distribution can't be reliably verified, or non-standard test statistics without an established theoretical null distribution) — a bioinformatician should understand permutation testing as a genuinely useful, more assumption-flexible alternative in these situations, while also recognizing its own practical limitation of requiring sufficient computational resources to perform enough permutations for a reliable, sufficiently fine-grained p-value estimate, particularly for very small p-values requiring a correspondingly very large number of permutations to estimate reliably.

### 5.7 🟢 What is the difference between a parametric and a non-parametric statistical test, and can you give an example of when a bioinformatician might specifically choose a non-parametric approach (such as a Wilcoxon rank-sum test) over its more commonly-used parametric counterpart (such as a t-test)?

A parametric statistical test makes specific assumptions about the underlying probability distribution of the data being analyzed (commonly assuming an approximately normal distribution), and its statistical validity depends on these assumptions being reasonably well met by the actual data. A non-parametric test instead makes fewer or less restrictive distributional assumptions (often working with the rank order of the data rather than its specific numerical values), generally trading some statistical power (when the parametric test's assumptions genuinely do hold) for greater robustness to violations of those assumptions. A bioinformatician might specifically choose a non-parametric test like the Wilcoxon rank-sum test over a standard t-test when working with a dataset that shows clear evidence of not being approximately normally distributed (a common situation for certain kinds of biological measurement data, particularly with smaller sample sizes where distributional assumptions are harder to verify confidently), or when the dataset contains outliers that a parametric test's normal-distribution assumption would be particularly sensitive to and potentially distorted by, since rank-based non-parametric approaches are generally more robust to the influence of extreme outlier values than tests that work directly with the raw numerical values.

### 5.8 🟡 What is the concept of an experimental design's statistical "factors" and "levels," and why might a bioinformatician recommend a factorial experimental design over a series of separate, simpler pairwise comparisons when a researcher wants to study the effects of two or more independent experimental variables simultaneously?

In experimental design terminology, a factor is an independent variable being deliberately manipulated or studied in an experiment (such as "drug treatment" or "genotype"), and a level is a specific value or category that factor can take (such as "treated" versus "untreated" for a drug treatment factor). A factorial experimental design deliberately and systematically combines multiple factors together within a single, integrated experiment (testing all, or a structured subset of, the possible combinations of each factor's levels) rather than running a series of separate, simpler experiments each testing only one factor at a time. A bioinformatician might recommend this kind of factorial design specifically because it offers a genuine, meaningful advantage beyond simple efficiency — a properly designed factorial experiment can directly test for and detect interaction effects (situations where one factor's effect genuinely depends on the specific level of a different factor, such as a drug treatment having a different effect in one genetic background compared to another), which a series of separate, single-factor experiments run independently simply cannot detect at all, since interaction effects are, by definition, only observable when multiple factors are studied together within the same coherent experimental structure — this is a genuinely important consideration for a bioinformatician to raise proactively during experimental design consultation, since a researcher focused primarily on their specific biological question may not always spontaneously consider this statistical design implication on their own.

### 5.9 🟢 What is the concept of statistical "effect size," and why is reporting effect size alongside statistical significance considered increasingly important best practice, beyond the general statistical-versus-biological significance distinction discussed throughout this repo?

Effect size is a quantitative measure of the actual magnitude of an observed effect or difference, distinct from and complementary to statistical significance, which only indicates whether an effect is unlikely to be due to chance without directly conveying how large or biologically meaningful that effect actually is. Reporting effect size alongside statistical significance has become increasingly emphasized best practice because, as discussed in adjacent precision medicine and longevity therapeutics biostatistics literature, a statistically significant finding with a very small effect size may have limited genuine biological or practical importance, particularly in large-sample-size genomics studies where even quite subtle effects can readily achieve statistical significance — reporting the actual effect size (such as fold-change in a differential expression context, or a correlation coefficient's actual magnitude in a different analytical context) alongside the p-value gives a reader the additional, genuinely necessary information needed to judge a finding's real-world importance and relevance, rather than relying on statistical significance alone as an incomplete and potentially misleading proxy for a finding's genuine biological meaningfulness.

### 5.10 🟡 What is bootstrapping as a statistical resampling technique, and how does its underlying logic differ from the permutation testing approach discussed in category 5.6, given that both involve repeatedly resampling from the observed data?

Bootstrapping repeatedly resamples the observed dataset with replacement (meaning a given data point can be selected more than once, or not at all, in any given resampled dataset) to build an empirical estimate of a statistic's sampling variability (such as constructing a confidence interval around an estimated parameter), whereas permutation testing (discussed in category 5.6) repeatedly reshuffles or reassigns labels within the data specifically to generate an empirical null distribution for hypothesis testing purposes. These techniques serve genuinely different statistical purposes despite both relying on repeated resampling from the same underlying dataset — bootstrapping is primarily used to estimate the uncertainty or variability around a statistic's true value (answering "how precisely can I estimate this quantity given my available data"), while permutation testing is primarily used to test whether an observed pattern is unlikely to have arisen by chance under a null hypothesis of no genuine effect (answering "is this specific observed result unlikely to be due to chance alone") — a bioinformatician needs to understand this distinction clearly to select the appropriate resampling approach for a given specific analytical question, rather than treating "resampling-based methods" as a single, undifferentiated statistical technique interchangeable for any purpose.

### 5.11 🟢 What is the concept of a confidence interval, and why does a bioinformatician communicating a point estimate (such as an estimated fold-change or effect size) without an accompanying confidence interval risk conveying a misleadingly false sense of precision?

A confidence interval provides a range of plausible values for an unknown true parameter, calculated in a way that, under repeated sampling, would be expected to contain the true parameter value a specified proportion of the time (commonly 95%), providing a direct, quantitative sense of the genuine uncertainty surrounding a given point estimate. Communicating only a single point estimate (such as reporting a gene showed "a 2.3-fold change" without any accompanying uncertainty measure) risks conveying a misleadingly false sense of precision, since a point estimate on its own gives no indication of how much genuine statistical uncertainty actually surrounds that specific number — a fold-change estimate of 2.3 derived from a very small, noisy dataset with a wide confidence interval (say, ranging from 1.1 to 4.8) represents genuinely different and much weaker evidence than the same point estimate of 2.3 derived from a larger, more precise dataset with a narrow confidence interval (say, 2.1 to 2.5), even though both would be reported with the identical headline point-estimate number — a rigorous bioinformatician should default to reporting and clearly communicating appropriate uncertainty measures alongside any point estimate, rather than presenting a single number in isolation in a way that obscures how much genuine confidence the underlying data actually supports.

### 5.12 🟡 What is the concept of a power law or heavy-tailed distribution, and why is understanding this specific type of distribution particularly relevant for a bioinformatician working with sequencing read count or network-based biological data, which frequently deviate substantially from a normal (Gaussian) distribution?

A power law or heavy-tailed distribution is a probability distribution where extreme, far-from-average values occur considerably more frequently than a normal (Gaussian) distribution would predict, with a small number of very large outlier values contributing disproportionately to the overall distribution's characteristics — a pattern that occurs commonly across various types of biological data a bioinformatician regularly encounters, including gene expression levels (where a relatively small number of genes are expressed at very high levels while most genes show comparatively low expression), and network-based biological data such as protein-protein interaction networks (where a small number of "hub" proteins have disproportionately many interactions compared to most other proteins in the network). Understanding this specific distributional pattern matters practically because many standard statistical methods and default analytical assumptions are built around normal-distribution expectations, and naively applying these methods to genuinely heavy-tailed biological data without appropriate adjustment (such as an appropriate data transformation, per the log-transformation commonly applied to gene expression data specifically because of this kind of skewed distribution, or using statistical methods specifically robust to this kind of distributional shape) can produce misleading or statistically invalid results — a bioinformatician should recognize when a specific dataset's distributional shape genuinely deviates from standard assumptions and should select or adjust analytical methods accordingly, rather than defaulting to methods built around normal-distribution assumptions without first checking whether this assumption is actually reasonably appropriate for the specific data at hand.

### 5.13 🟢 What is the difference between a one-tailed and a two-tailed statistical test, and why should a bioinformatician generally default to a two-tailed test unless there's a specific, well-justified, and ideally pre-specified reason to expect an effect only in one particular direction?

A two-tailed test evaluates whether an observed effect differs significantly from the null hypothesis in either direction (either meaningfully higher or meaningfully lower than expected), while a one-tailed test specifically evaluates only one particular direction of effect, generally offering somewhat greater statistical power to detect an effect in that specific pre-specified direction, but at the cost of being completely unable to detect (or even properly characterize) a genuine, real effect that happens to occur in the opposite direction from what was specified. A bioinformatician should generally default to a two-tailed test unless there's a specific, well-justified, and ideally explicitly pre-specified reason (established before seeing the data, echoing the pre-specification discipline discussed extensively in adjacent precision medicine and longevity therapeutics biostatistics literature) to expect an effect only in one particular direction, because choosing a one-tailed test specifically and conveniently after observing which direction an effect happens to trend in the actual data represents a form of the kind of post-hoc analytical flexibility and implicit multiple-testing that this same adjacent literature discusses as a serious source of inflated, unreliable statistical significance — a one-tailed test's legitimate use requires the directional hypothesis to be genuinely and specifically justified in advance on independent biological or mechanistic grounds, not selected for statistical convenience after the fact based on which direction the observed data happened to trend.

### 5.14 🔴 A researcher asks you to help design a genomics experiment but has a fixed, limited budget that constrains the total number of samples that can be sequenced. They want to maximize their chances of detecting genuinely important biological findings. How would you approach advising on the optimal allocation of this limited budget between increasing the number of biological replicates versus increasing sequencing depth per sample?

I'd approach this as a genuine, quantitative tradeoff analysis rather than defaulting to a fixed rule of thumb, since the optimal allocation actually depends on the specific biological question and expected effect characteristics. First, I'd want to understand the specific analytical goal — for many differential expression and variant discovery applications, the accumulated methodological literature and empirical benchmarking in this field has generally found that, once a reasonably adequate minimum sequencing depth per sample is achieved (sufficient to reliably detect and quantify the features of interest), statistical power for detecting a genuine biological effect tends to benefit more from additional biological replicates than from further increases in per-sample sequencing depth beyond this adequate threshold, since biological replicates directly address the biological variability (per the negative binomial overdispersion discussion in category 4.6, and the biological-versus-technical replicate distinction in category 5.1) that fundamentally limits statistical power for detecting a genuine, biologically real effect across a population, whereas additional sequencing depth primarily improves the precision of measurement for each individual sample without addressing this more fundamental biological variability source at all. Given this generally-established principle, I'd typically recommend prioritizing a reasonably higher number of biological replicates over pushing sequencing depth substantially beyond an already-adequate threshold, within the fixed budget — but I'd want to validate this general guidance against the specific application at hand rather than applying it uninformed by the specific context, since some specific applications genuinely do require higher per-sample depth as a hard prerequisite regardless of replicate number (detecting rare, low-allele-fraction somatic variants, per the discussion in category 3.15, or accurately quantifying genuinely lowly-expressed genes or transcripts, both of which have a real minimum depth requirement below which no amount of additional replication can compensate for simply not having adequately sampled the feature of interest in any given sample at all) — I'd want to work through a proper power calculation (per category 5.2) using reasonable, ideally literature-informed or pilot-data-informed assumptions about expected effect size and variability for this specific study's actual biological question, comparing the projected power achievable under several different specific replicate-versus-depth allocation scenarios within the fixed total budget, and presenting the researcher with this concrete, quantified tradeoff comparison rather than a generic, one-size-fits-all recommendation not grounded in their study's actual specific parameters and goals.
