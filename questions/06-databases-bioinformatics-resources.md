# 6. Databases & Bioinformatics Resources

The major public databases, ontologies, and file format standards a working bioinformatician navigates daily.

---

### 6.1 🟢 What is the difference between a primary sequence database (like GenBank) and a curated, secondary database (like RefSeq or UniProt/Swiss-Prot), and why does this distinction matter for how much confidence to place in a given database entry?

A primary sequence database like GenBank accepts direct, largely uncurated submissions from researchers, meaning it aims to be comprehensive and inclusive of essentially all submitted sequence data, but doesn't apply the same level of expert review, quality filtering, or non-redundancy processing that a curated secondary database does — this means a primary database can contain sequences with genuine errors, incomplete annotation, or substantial redundancy (many near-identical or duplicate entries for the same underlying biological sequence). A curated secondary database like RefSeq (for nucleotide sequences) or UniProt/Swiss-Prot (for protein sequences) applies additional expert review and curation, aiming to provide a single, representative, quality-controlled reference sequence and annotation for a given gene or protein, generally with more reliable and consistent annotation than what's directly available from primary database entries alone. This distinction matters for how much confidence a bioinformatician should place in a given database entry — for most standard reference or annotation purposes, a curated resource like RefSeq or Swiss-Prot generally provides more reliable, consistent information, while a primary database like GenBank remains valuable specifically for its comprehensiveness and its role as the actual original submission archive, meaning a bioinformatician should choose the appropriate database based on whether comprehensiveness or curated reliability is the more important consideration for the specific task at hand.

### 6.2 🟡 What is Ensembl and how does its specific role and scope differ from and complement the NCBI/RefSeq ecosystem, given that a bioinformatician working with human or model organism genomic data will very commonly need to work with both?

Ensembl is a genome annotation and browsing resource, similar in general purpose to NCBI's genome and RefSeq resources, but developed and maintained by a genuinely separate organization (a collaboration between EMBL-EBI and the Wellcome Sanger Institute) using its own independent gene annotation pipeline and, in some respects, its own specific conventions and terminology — meaning Ensembl and RefSeq, while both providing gene annotation for many of the same organisms and often broadly agreeing on the major, well-established genes, can genuinely differ in specific details (particular transcript models, specific gene boundaries, or the inclusion of certain more speculative or lower-confidence annotations) due to their independent annotation processes and methodological choices. A bioinformatician working with human or model organism genomic data commonly needs familiarity with both resources specifically because different tools, published datasets, and collaborators may use either convention, and correctly cross-referencing and reconciling gene and transcript identifiers between these two ecosystems (which use genuinely different identifier naming schemes) is a routine, practically necessary skill — a bioinformatician should understand that "the" annotation for a given gene isn't a single, universally agreed-upon fixed entity, but can differ meaningfully depending on which specific annotation resource and version is being used, which has real implications for reproducibility when combining data or results derived from different annotation sources.

### 6.3 🟢 What is Gene Ontology (GO), and what are its three separate, distinct namespaces (or aspects) that a bioinformatician needs to understand are conceptually different categories of annotation, rather than a single unified classification?

Gene Ontology is a widely-used, structured, controlled vocabulary system for annotating gene products with standardized descriptions of their biological roles, organized into three deliberately separate namespaces representing conceptually distinct categories of annotation — Molecular Function (describing the specific biochemical activity a gene product performs, such as a specific enzymatic activity), Biological Process (describing the broader biological objective or pathway a gene product contributes to, such as "DNA repair" or "apoptosis"), and Cellular Component (describing where within the cell a gene product is typically located or active, such as "nucleus" or "mitochondrial membrane"). A bioinformatician needs to understand these as genuinely distinct, separately-structured namespaces rather than a single unified classification because a given gene product typically has separate, independent annotations within each of these three namespaces, and conflating or failing to distinguish which specific namespace a given GO term actually belongs to (particularly important when performing the gene set enrichment analysis discussed in category 4.9, which is very commonly performed using GO terms as the pre-defined gene sets) can lead to a confused or improperly structured analysis that doesn't correctly respect this deliberate, meaningful separation between what a gene product does biochemically, what broader process it participates in, and where it's physically located.

### 6.4 🟡 What is the GO term hierarchical (directed acyclic graph) structure, and why does correctly accounting for this hierarchy matter for accurate gene set enrichment analysis, beyond simply treating each GO term as an independent, unrelated category?

Gene Ontology terms are organized in a hierarchical structure — specifically a directed acyclic graph rather than a simple tree, meaning a more specific child term can have more than one parent term — where more specific, granular terms are related to broader, more general parent terms through defined relationship types (most commonly "is a" and "part of" relationships), and a gene annotated with a specific, granular GO term is implicitly also considered annotated with all of that term's more general ancestor terms as well, following what's called the true path rule. Correctly accounting for this hierarchical structure matters for accurate gene set enrichment analysis because failing to properly propagate annotations up through this hierarchy (or, conversely, failing to appropriately account for the statistical non-independence between hierarchically related terms, since a broad parent term's gene set necessarily overlaps substantially with all of its child terms' gene sets) can lead to either missed or statistically distorted enrichment results — a bioinformatician performing GO enrichment analysis should use tools specifically designed to correctly handle this hierarchical structure (many established enrichment tools incorporate specific methodology to account for this dependency, such as approaches that specifically test whether a term's enrichment signal is genuinely distinct from what would already be expected given its more general parent terms' enrichment) rather than treating each GO term as an entirely independent, unrelated category in a naive statistical test.

### 6.5 🟢 What is KEGG, and how does its focus on curated metabolic and signaling pathway maps differ in scope and typical use case from the more general-purpose functional annotation provided by Gene Ontology?

KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) is a database resource centered specifically around curated, visually-represented biological pathway maps — depicting specific metabolic pathways, signaling cascades, and other structured biological processes with genes and their products mapped onto specific positions and relationships within these defined pathway diagrams — differing in scope from Gene Ontology's more general-purpose, broadly applicable functional annotation vocabulary discussed in category 6.3. This difference in scope leads to a genuinely different typical use case — KEGG pathway analysis is particularly valuable when a researcher wants to understand a specific gene or gene set's role within a well-defined, structurally mapped biological pathway context (understanding exactly where in a specific metabolic cascade a given enzyme acts, for instance, or visualizing how a set of differentially expressed genes maps onto a specific, coherent signaling pathway diagram), while GO annotation and enrichment is generally more useful for a broader, less structurally specific characterization of a gene set's general functional themes across the full breadth of biological function, process, and localization categories — a bioinformatician performing functional characterization of a gene list will often use both resources together, since they provide genuinely complementary rather than redundant perspectives on gene function.

### 6.6 🟡 What is the Sequence Read Archive (SRA), and what specific practical challenge does a bioinformatician commonly encounter when trying to reproduce or reuse a previously-published analysis based on raw sequencing data deposited there?

The Sequence Read Archive is a major public repository for raw, unprocessed sequencing data (and associated experimental metadata), hosted by NCBI (with mirrored, internationally coordinated equivalents at EBI's European Nucleotide Archive and Japan's DDBJ), serving as the standard deposit location for raw sequencing data supporting published research, generally required by most major journals and funding agencies as a condition of publication. A specific practical challenge a bioinformatician commonly encounters when trying to reproduce or reuse SRA-deposited data is that the associated experimental metadata (describing exactly what each specific sample and sequencing run actually represents — which experimental condition, which specific replicate, and other relevant experimental design details) is often incompletely, inconsistently, or ambiguously documented by the original depositing researchers, since metadata submission standards and enforcement have historically been considerably less rigorous and standardized than the raw sequence data submission itself — a bioinformatician attempting to reuse this kind of publicly deposited data for a new analysis (including, commonly, a meta-analysis combining data across multiple independent studies) needs to budget realistic time and effort for this often underappreciated metadata reconciliation and validation work, and should be appropriately cautious about relying on metadata labels at face value without some degree of independent verification or sanity-checking against the associated publication's actual described methodology.

### 6.7 🟢 What is the difference between GRCh37 (hg19) and GRCh38 (hg38) as human reference genome assembly versions, and why is confirming which specific assembly version a given dataset or coordinate set uses such a routine, essential first step before combining or comparing genomic data from different sources?

GRCh37 (also commonly referred to by its UCSC designation, hg19) and GRCh38 (hg38) are two different, successive major versions of the human reference genome assembly, with GRCh38 representing a more recent, improved assembly incorporating various corrections, gap closures, and the alt-contig representation of structurally variable regions discussed in category 1.9. Confirming which specific assembly version a given dataset uses is such a routine, essential first step before combining or comparing genomic data from different sources because genomic coordinates are assembly-version-specific — the exact same genomic position (chromosome and base-pair number) can refer to a genuinely different actual genomic location depending on which assembly version the coordinate is being interpreted against, since the underlying sequence and its coordinate numbering can shift between assembly versions, particularly in regions that were specifically corrected or restructured between versions — combining or comparing genomic coordinate data from sources using different, unreconciled assembly versions without appropriate coordinate conversion (using a dedicated "liftover" tool specifically designed to convert coordinates between assembly versions) is a genuinely common and consequential error, since the resulting analysis would be silently comparing or combining data that actually refers to different underlying genomic locations despite having superficially matching coordinate numbers.

### 6.8 🟡 What is a VCF (Variant Call Format) file, and what specific pieces of information does its structure separate into distinct sections to efficiently represent variant calls across potentially many samples simultaneously?

A VCF file is the standard file format for representing genetic variants and their associated genotype calls, structured to efficiently represent variant information across potentially many samples simultaneously within a single file, discussed briefly in adjacent precision medicine genomics literature. Its structure separates information into distinct, purposeful sections — a header section documenting the file's metadata and defining the meaning of any custom annotation fields used elsewhere in the file; a set of fixed columns for each variant record describing the variant's position, reference and alternate alleles, and various site-level quality and annotation information common to that variant regardless of which specific samples carry it; and, for multi-sample VCF files, a genotype section with one column per sample, encoding each individual sample's specific genotype and sample-level quality metrics at that variant position — a bioinformatician needs working fluency with this format's specific structure and conventions (including understanding how missing data, multi-allelic sites, and phased versus unphased genotype information are each represented) since VCF is the near-universal standard interchange format for variant data throughout essentially the entire genomics pipeline ecosystem discussed throughout category 3.

### 6.9 🟢 What is the difference between the BAM and CRAM file formats for storing aligned sequencing reads, and what specific tradeoff does CRAM's typically smaller file size come with?

BAM (Binary Alignment Map) is the standard binary format for storing aligned sequencing reads, representing a compressed version of the human-readable SAM (Sequence Alignment Map) text format. CRAM is a more recently developed, alternative binary alignment storage format specifically designed to achieve smaller file sizes than BAM, primarily by more aggressively leveraging the fact that most bases in an aligned read match the reference genome sequence at that position, and therefore don't need to be stored explicitly at all if the reference sequence being used is available — instead, CRAM can store primarily just the specific positions where a read differs from the reference, reconstructing the full read sequence on demand by combining this difference information with the reference sequence. This more aggressive compression comes with a specific tradeoff a bioinformatician needs to be aware of — because CRAM's compression scheme depends on having access to the exact same specific reference sequence that was originally used to create the file, a CRAM file isn't fully self-contained the way a BAM file effectively is, and correctly reading a CRAM file requires ensuring the exact correct, matching reference sequence is available and correctly specified, which introduces an additional dependency and potential point of failure (particularly around reference version mismatches, echoing the assembly version concern discussed in category 6.7) that BAM files don't have in quite the same way, even though CRAM's storage efficiency advantage is often substantial enough to be well worth this added dependency management consideration for large-scale sequencing data storage.

### 6.10 🟡 What is a genome browser (such as the UCSC Genome Browser or IGV), and why does direct visual inspection of aligned sequencing data in a genome browser remain an important, non-obsolete practice even in an era of highly automated variant calling pipelines?

A genome browser is a software tool that provides interactive, visual representation of genomic sequence, annotation, and aligned sequencing read data along genomic coordinates, allowing a user to directly and visually inspect a specific genomic region of interest at whatever level of detail is needed, from a whole-chromosome overview down to individual base-pair-level read alignment detail. Direct visual inspection in a genome browser remains an important, non-obsolete practice even given highly automated pipelines because it provides a genuinely different and valuable kind of validation than any automated summary statistic or filtering criterion can fully replace — a bioinformatician can directly and intuitively assess whether a specific called variant's supporting reads actually look convincing (checking for the kind of subtle alignment artifacts, strand bias, or other visually apparent quality issues that might not be fully captured by a pipeline's automated quality filters) or whether an unexpected or surprising result actually reflects a genuine biological finding versus some kind of technical artifact that becomes immediately obvious once the underlying raw read data is actually visually examined — this kind of direct visual "sanity check" of specific, particularly important or surprising findings remains a genuinely important quality control practice that experienced bioinformaticians routinely apply, rather than relying entirely on automated pipeline output without ever directly examining the underlying raw evidence for at least the most consequential specific findings.

### 6.11 🟢 What is the difference between GFF/GTF file formats and BED file format, and when would a bioinformatician typically use each?

GFF (General Feature Format) and GTF (Gene Transfer Format, a more constrained, gene-annotation-specific variant of GFF) are file formats for representing genomic feature annotations — genes, transcripts, exons, and their hierarchical relationships to each other — commonly used for representing a genome's structural gene annotation, including the transcript and exon structure information needed for the splice-aware alignment and RNA-seq quantification discussed throughout category 4. BED (Browser Extensible Data) format is a simpler, more general-purpose format for representing genomic intervals (a defined start and end coordinate on a specific chromosome), without the hierarchical gene/transcript/exon structure that GFF/GTF specifically encode, commonly used for representing simpler sets of genomic regions of interest that don't require this more complex hierarchical structure — a set of ChIP-seq peak regions, a target capture panel's covered regions, or any other simple list of genomic intervals. A bioinformatician would typically use GFF/GTF specifically when the hierarchical gene/transcript/exon structure itself is analytically relevant (as it is for splice-aware RNA-seq analysis), and would use the simpler BED format when only a flat list of genomic intervals is needed without this additional hierarchical structure, since using an unnecessarily complex format for a simpler task adds needless complexity without providing any actual analytical benefit.

### 6.12 🔴 A bioinformatician needs to combine variant call data from three different published studies, each of which used a different reference genome assembly version and a different variant annotation source, to perform a combined meta-analysis. What specific steps and potential pitfalls should this reconciliation process account for?

I'd approach this systematically, treating proper data harmonization as a genuinely substantial and necessary prerequisite step before any actual combined analysis could be considered valid, rather than a minor technical formality. First, per the assembly version discussion in category 6.7, I'd need to confirm exactly which specific reference assembly version each study's variant coordinates are reported against, and use an appropriate, validated liftover tool to convert all three datasets to a single, common assembly version before any direct coordinate comparison — I'd want to specifically check the liftover process's own success rate and flag any variants that failed to convert cleanly (which can happen for variants falling in regions that were substantially restructured between assembly versions), rather than assuming liftover always succeeds cleanly and silently proceeding with an incomplete or subtly corrupted converted dataset. Second, per the annotation source discussion in category 6.2, if the three studies used different underlying gene annotation sources (Ensembl versus RefSeq, for instance) for describing variant consequences or affected genes, I'd need to reconcile gene and transcript identifiers across these different naming conventions, being alert to cases where the specific gene boundaries or transcript models genuinely differ between annotation sources in ways that could affect how a given variant is characterized or attributed to a specific gene. Third, I'd want to check whether the three studies used comparable variant calling and filtering methodology (per the discussion throughout category 3) — even after resolving the coordinate and annotation reconciliation, meaningfully different variant calling pipelines, filtering thresholds, or sequencing technologies across the three studies could introduce genuine, confounding methodological heterogeneity into any combined analysis, similar in spirit to the batch effect concerns discussed in categories 4.8 and 5.3, and I'd want to at least characterize and transparently document this heterogeneity even if it can't be fully eliminated, rather than treating the three combined datasets as though they were generated through a single, uniform methodology. Fourth, I'd want to verify allele representation consistency — different variant callers and annotation pipelines can sometimes represent the same underlying biological variant using different but formally equivalent notation (particularly for indels, which can often be legitimately represented in more than one way depending on how the alignment algorithm resolves ambiguous positioning), meaning a naive, purely string-based comparison of variant identifiers across the three datasets could fail to recognize genuinely identical variants as matching, requiring proper variant normalization (a standard preprocessing step specifically designed to resolve this kind of representational ambiguity into a single, canonical form) before any cross-dataset comparison or combination. And throughout, I'd want to maintain clear, thorough documentation of every specific harmonization and conversion step applied, both for the sake of the analysis's own reproducibility and so that any downstream reviewer or collaborator can understand and, if needed, independently verify or reproduce this substantial and consequential data reconciliation process, rather than presenting the final combined dataset as though it required no meaningful preprocessing effort to produce.
